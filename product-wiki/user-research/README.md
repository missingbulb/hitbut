# User research — the four target segments

## Key insights

- Backfire effect failed to replicate: 10,000+ subjects, 52 issues, zero backfires — corrections do move beliefs
- Fact-checker trust splits 70/29 by party; users skew liberal and educated, so a "neutral" tool inherits an asymmetric audience
- Demand is spiky: "fact check" searches +650% during one debate; a candidate's Wikipedia page hit 450K views/day pre-primary
- Supply is shrinking as demand grows: ~30 fact-checking projects died vs 10 born in 2025; 76% of orgs call finances vulnerable
- US newspapers lost 75%+ of jobs since 2005; full-time statehouse reporters fell 374→245 — nobody is keeping the record
- Twitter killed free academic API in 2023 ($42K/mo replacement); PhD students changed topics — political-text corpora are starved
- Oppo tracking is already industrial: American Bridge ran 44 trackers on ~$18M/yr; a single oppo book costs $10K–50K

## General public / engaged citizens

**Trust in fact-checkers is deeply partisan.** Pew found 70% of Republicans say fact-checkers "tend to favor one side" vs 29% of Democrats — a 41-point gap; overall Americans split roughly 50/48 on whether fact-checkers deal fairly ([Pew Research, 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/06/27/republicans-far-more-likely-than-democrats-to-say-fact-checkers-tend-to-favor-one-side/)). The academic literature confirms the audience skew: awareness, positive attitudes and self-reported use of fact-checking sites concentrate among liberals, mainstream-news consumers, and the more educated ([Robertson, Mourão & Thorson 2020, International Journal of Press/Politics](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1940161219898055)); Republicans are more skeptical of all fact-check label types ([HKS Misinformation Review](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/journalistic-interventions-matter-understanding-how-americans-perceive-fact-checking-labels/)). Implication for a browse-anyone tool: neutrality of method does not buy a neutral audience.

**The backfire effect is largely debunked — corrections work.** Wood and Porter's replication attempts (5 experiments, 10,000+ participants, 52 issues) found no items triggering backfire ([ScienceDirect review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211368120300516)); Nyhan and co-authors now argue durability of misperceptions is not explained by backfire ([PNAS 2020](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1912440117)); Full Fact's own review reached the same conclusion ([Full Fact report, PDF](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/backfire_report_fullfact.pdf)). Subjects even accept corrections of allied politicians, though belief improvements are modest.

**Demand is event-driven and spiky, not steady.** Google searches for "fact check" spiked +650% during a November 2019 Democratic debate ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/campaign/471437-heres-what-searches-spiked-on-google-in-response-to-democrats-debate/)), and a broad debate fact-check was among the top queries during a 2024 VP debate ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/campaigns/presidential/3174106/what-people-were-searching-vp-debate/)). The same pattern shows on Wikipedia: candidate pageviews surge just before elections and correlate with outcomes ([EPJ Data Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0083-3); [Washington Post on the mechanism](https://www.washingtonpost.com/graphics/politics/2016-election/presidential-wikipedias/)), with Nikki Haley's page reportedly reaching ~450K views/day around the 2024 early contests (traffic figures per search-result coverage; not independently verified). Baseline fact-check site traffic is modest between events — per Ahrefs, PolitiFact saw ~122K visits in May 2025 vs Snopes ~252K ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/websites/politifact.com/competitors); analytics-vendor estimate, not publisher-reported). Reuters Institute's 2025 report adds that when people verify suspicious content they go first to trusted news brands, but increasingly also to AI chatbots and social media — a "flatter" verification hierarchy ([DNR 2025 executive summary](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)); 58% globally worry about telling real from fake news, 73% in the US ([full PDF](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-06/Digital_News-Report_2025.pdf)).

## Journalists and fact-checkers

**The claim-finding step is already partially automated — by the fact-checking community itself.** Duke Reporters' Lab's Tech & Check runs ClaimBuster over transcripts and social posts and emails fact-checkers daily lists of checkable statements; its Fact-Check Insights database holds structured data on 180,000+ checked claims via the ClaimReview schema ([Duke Sanford](https://sanford.duke.edu/blog-post/duke-reporters-lab-gives-fact-checkers-researchers-new-tools-thwart-misinformation/)). This is the strongest signal of what the bottleneck is: finding and re-finding what a figure said, at scale, across sources.

**Their current tool stack is fragmented and expensive.** Broadcast monitoring runs through TVEyes (2.5M+ hours of broadcast/month, real-time transcripts and alerts — [TVEyes](https://www.tveyes.com/)) and clip marketplaces like Grabien, which monitors live events and sells professionally cut, transcribed clips ([Grabien](https://grabien.com/)). Print archive search means Nexis, historically ~$600/month per seat — a freelancer-negotiated group deal at $26/month was newsworthy in itself ([Tools for Reporters](https://toolsforreporters.com/2021/03/24/a-nexis-for-the-rest-of-us/)). Transcription is commodity, with the key journalist feature being timestamp-linked audio for quote verification ([The Media Copilot](https://mediacopilot.ai/the-best-ai-transcription-tools-for-journalists-hands-on-review/)). C-SPAN's archive — 200,000+ hours since 1987, word-level searchable — is the free gold standard but covers only official proceedings and its own programming ([Purdue researcher guide, PDF](https://cla.purdue.edu/communication/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/How-To-Guide-for-Researchers-C-SPAN-Archives.pdf)).

**The people doing this work are disappearing.** 270,000+ US newspaper jobs gone since 2005 (75%+); 1,200 newspaper and 1,800 digital-publisher journalist jobs lost in the past year alone ([Medill State of Local News 2025](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2025/report/); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/10/in-medills-latest-state-of-local-news-report-a-festering-20-year-old-problem-looms-larger-than-ever/)). Full-time newspaper statehouse reporters fell from 374 (2014) to 245 (2022) ([Pew media layoffs topic page](https://www.pewresearch.org/topic/news-habits-media/news-media-trends/media-industry/media-layoffs-employment/)). Fact-checking itself is contracting: Duke's census counted 443 active projects in 2025, down from 451, with ~30 closures vs 10 launches, as Meta ended its US third-party program ([Duke census — reporterslab.org is egress-blocked from this environment; figures per search results attributing them to the Duke Reporters' Lab censuses](https://reporterslab.org/2025/06/19/fact-checkers-persevere-as-politicians-platforms-turn-up-heat/); see also [Poynter on the 2026 census](https://www.poynter.org/fact-checking/2026/fact-checking-projects-survive-funding-cuts-duke-census/)). IFCN's State of the Fact-Checkers surveys report 76% of organizations describing finances as vulnerable or in crisis even while 62% grew audiences, and ~72% facing harassment ([Rappler on the 2025 report](https://www.rappler.com/world/global-affairs/state-fact-checkers-report-2025-ifcn/); [Poynter on the 2023 report](https://www.poynter.org/ifcn/2024/state-fact-checkers-report-2023-findings/)). A CHI 2025 study documents fact-checkers' explicit requirement that any automated assistance show its evidence trail — opaque verdicts are unusable to them ([ACM CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713277)).

## Researchers and academics

**Today's corpora are institution-centric, not person-centric.** The workhorses: UCSB's American Presidency Project (163,161 records as of 2024, presidents 1789–present, run by two academics since 1999 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_Presidency_Project); [APP about page](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/about)); ParlSpeech V2 (6.3M parliamentary speeches, 9 democracies, harmonized metadata, on Harvard Dataverse — [Dataverse](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/L4OAKN)); UK Hansard scraped from official HTML. All cover formal settings (chamber floor, official documents); interviews, rallies, podcasts and TV hits — where contradictions actually surface — are largely uncorpused.

**The Twitter API shutdown left a documented data vacuum.** Free academic access ended early 2023; the replacement enterprise tier started at $42,000/month, halting 100+ active projects and 250+ planned ones and pushing doctoral students to change topics ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91040397/under-elon-musk-x-is-denying-api-access-to-academics-who-study-misinformation); [Coalition for Independent Technology Research letter](https://independenttechresearch.org/letter-twitters-new-api-plans-will-devastate-public-interest-research/); ["RIP Twitter API" eulogy, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07340)). There is still no academic tier in 2026 ([xcrop.io overview](https://xcrop.io/blog/twitter-data-academic-research)). A stable, exportable statements corpus lands directly in this gap.

**Replication norms make stable identifiers and dumps a hard requirement, not a nicety.** AJPS has required open replication data since 2012; sharing rose from ~8% of political-science articles in 2012 to 31% in 2022 — meaning 69% still don't, partly because sources are hard to package ([PS: Political Science & Politics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/data-and-code-availability-in-political-science-publications-from-1995-to-2022/F95EA30BEDC1B7A52CAE143D332B0746); [AJPS guidelines, PDF](https://ajps.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/ajps_replication-guidelines-2-1.pdf)). Harvard Dataverse is the de facto deposit venue. Separately, stance detection over political text is an active NLP subfield with its own datasets (P-STANCE, SemEval-2016), but tracking one figure's position *changes over time* is noted as under-served ([LLM stance-detection survey, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08464); [Cambridge PSRM practical guide](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/stance-detection-a-practical-guide-to-classifying-political-beliefs-in-text/E227E746BD7D9751526DA0EC2C378787)) — hitbut's contradiction layer is itself a publishable dataset.

## Campaign and advocacy staff

**"Tracking" is a physical, industrial practice with real budgets.** American Bridge (D) grew from 20 trackers in 2012 to 44 trackers in 41 states on ~$18M/year; America Rising (R) ran ~$8M/year with 27 full-time trackers in 24 states — twenty-somethings filming targets' every public utterance, with footage analyzed and archived at DC-area headquarters ([Yahoo/HuffPost](https://news.yahoo.com/down-candidate-era-nothing-matters-201024378.html); [Slate on America Rising](https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2014/01/america-rising-the-republican-pac-that-s-filming-every-vulnerable-democratic-candidate.html); [PBS Frontline on the rise of the "campaign stalker" post-Citizens United](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/frontline/article/citizens-united-and-the-rise-of-the-campaign-stalker/)). America Rising even monetized its archive by selling candidate photos and research to paying clients ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/america-rising-trackers-to-sell-candidate-photos-at-a-competitive-price)).

**Oppo research is bought per-book, at prices that make an automated corpus look free.** A local/statewide oppo book runs $10,000–$50,000; one firm publicly prices a "Core Book" at $19,375–$23,250 depending on turnaround ([Nesbitt & Parrinello pricing page](https://www.nesbittresearch.com/opposition-research-price/)). Candidates also commission "self-research" books to know their own vulnerabilities ([The Campaign Workshop](https://www.thecampaignworkshop.com/blog/advocacy-campaigns/opposition-research)). The war-room workflow this feeds — rapid response, clip cutting, flip-flop hunting — is serviced today by Grabien's TVClipper and live-event monitoring ([Grabien TVClipper](https://grabien.com/tvclipper)).

**Media monitoring is a multi-billion-dollar spend category.** 2025 market-size estimates from research firms cluster at $5.4–6.3B globally, ~11–12% CAGR, North America ~37% of it ([Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/media-monitoring-market); [Fortune Business Insights](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/media-monitoring-tools-market-104157); vendor market-research estimates — origins differ, treat as a range).

## Cross-cutting

- **The precedent product is editorial, not computational.** PolitiFact's Flip-O-Meter (launched 2008: No Flip / Half Flip / Full Flop) is exactly hitbut's consistency use case, done by hand per-claim, explicitly framed as informational rather than judgmental ([PolitiFact](https://www.politifact.com/article/2008/aug/05/introducing-flip-o-meter/)). It has no corpus, no API, no alerts.
- **Supply-demand scissors.** Verification demand spikes around events while paid human capacity (newsrooms, fact-check orgs, oppo shops) shrinks or stays boutique — the structural opening for an automated, citable corpus serving all four segments from one backend.
- **Show-the-work is the universal requirement.** Fact-checkers demand evidence trails from automation ([CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713277)), academics demand replication files, and public-trust research says the partisan audience gap is the main adoption risk — per-statement sourcing is the one feature every segment names.
