# Market landscape — civic accountability / fact-checking tech

## Key insights

- Knesset proceedings carry no copyright at all under Copyright Act §6 — the Hebrew corpus backbone is free where C-SPAN's is closed
- The Knesset's OData API needs no key, lists 38 collections and links every sitting's protocol file — verified live this pass
- Every sitting is published twice — the edited "דברי הכנסת" and the raw stenogram — and the two texts do not match
- Protocol files carry inline speaker tags (`<< יור >>`, `<< דובר >>`), so speaker segmentation is given, not inferred
- Israel has exactly one IFCN-accredited fact-checker — Globes' HaMashrokit — publishing roughly one item a day
- Israeli defamation law awards damages without proof of harm, and truth alone is no defence: it must also serve the public interest
- Hebrew NLP is largely pre-paid by nonprofits — DictaLM, Knesset-DictaBERT and ivrit.ai's 10,000 speech hours are all open

## Israel is the market, and it is small, contested and on a clock

The product is Hebrew and Israeli. Everything below this section, down to `## Sources`, was researched against the **US** field and is retained as transferable background — the funding shocks, the platform-dependence lessons, the corpus traps — not as a description of this market. Where a US finding has an Israeli counterpart, the Israeli one is here.

**Scale is the first constraint.** Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics put the population at 10.178 million at the start of 2026 — 7.771 million Jews and others (76.3%), 2.147 million Arabs (21.1%), 0.26 million foreign nationals ([CBS figures as reported by israel.com](https://israel.com/public/israel-starts-2026-with-more-than-10-million-people/)). A Hebrew-language civic product addresses at most that, and realistically the Hebrew-reading share of it. Every US comparison on this page is drawn from a market roughly thirty times larger, so US revenue and traffic figures transfer as *shape*, never as magnitude.

**There is a fixed date to build toward.** The next Knesset election is 27 October 2026 — the last date legally available, and the first time since 1988 that a Knesset will have served a full term ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/judge-sets-date-for-next-scheduled-elections-for-october-2026/), [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/12/israel-to-hold-national-elections-on-october-27-parliament-says)). The demand spike a US election produces for fact-checking tools (see the user-research wiki) has an Israeli equivalent with a known date, and the 25th Knesset's full term also means an unusually long, continuous record of the same figures speaking — four years of one parliament rather than the fragmented 2019–2022 sequence.

**The supply of professional fact-checking is one desk.** Israel has exactly one IFCN-accredited fact-checking organisation: **HaMashrokit** (המשרוקית, "The Whistle"), an independent unit inside the business daily *Globes*, an IFCN signatory since 2018 and a Meta third-party partner before that ([IFCN Code of Principles listing](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/application/public/the-whistle/661a8b0fba7689d48142c10b), [Times of Israel on the Facebook partnership](https://www.timesofisrael.com/facebook-partners-with-hebrew-fact-checkers-as-campaign-rhetoric-heats-up/)). It runs one item a day, has published 300+ checks since its July 2017 launch, and moved into *Globes* in January 2019 after two years as an NGO ([Globes, about HaMashrokit](https://www.globes.co.il/news/article.aspx?did=1001372882)). Against a US field of hundreds of organisations, Israel's entire professional fact-checking capacity is a single newsroom unit — which makes the field a potential partner rather than a competitor, and makes the coverage gap enormous.

## Where a Hebrew statements corpus can lawfully come from

This is where the Israeli picture is structurally *better* than the US one this page describes above.

**Knesset proceedings are outside copyright by statute.** Section 6 of the Copyright Act 5768–2007 reads: *"על אף הוראות סעיף 4, לא תהא זכות יוצרים בחוקים, בתקנות, בדברי הכנסת ובהחלטות שיפוטיות"* — no copyright subsists in laws, regulations, **the proceedings of the Knesset**, or judicial decisions ([full text of the law, Nevo](https://www.nevo.co.il/law_html/law00/3953.htm)). The US equivalent had to be assembled from a public-domain argument about federal works plus a private archive's terms of use; here the exemption is written into the statute and names parliamentary proceedings explicitly. The phrase the statute exempts, "דברי הכנסת", is the same phrase the Knesset's own document system uses as the type name for the published record (below) — the exemption and the artefact line up exactly.

**The Knesset publishes it through an open API, no key, no registration.** `https://knesset.gov.il/Odata/ParliamentInfo.svc/` is a live OData service exposing 38 collections — bills, committees, sittings, people, factions, positions, and crucially `KNS_DocumentPlenumSession` and `KNS_DocumentCommitteeSession`, which carry the document URLs for each sitting. It answers JSON with `$format=json` and supports ordinary OData query options. *Verified first-hand on 2026-08-23: the service document returned HTTP 200 with 38 collections, and `KNS_Person?$top=1&$format=json` returned a person record.* A document row looks like this (fetched the same day):

```json
{"DocumentPlenumSessionID": "7716797", "PlenumSessionID": 2233500,
 "GroupTypeDesc": "דברי הכנסת", "ApplicationDesc": "DOC",
 "FilePath": "https://fs.knesset.gov.il/25/Plenum/25_ptm_7716797.doc",
 "LastUpdatedDate": "2026-08-16T14:02:00"}
```

Third-party documentation of the same service is thin and partly second-hand; a community skill description states that per-MK voting is *not* exposed by this API and that data before the 18th Knesset is excluded ([knesset-legislative-tracker skill listing](https://lobehub.com/zh/skills/skills-il-government-services-knesset-legislative-tracker)) — unverified here, and an open question below. The Public Knowledge Workshop's long-running [Open Knesset](https://oknesset.org/) project and its [`hasadna/knesset-data`](https://github.com/hasadna/knesset-data) libraries wrap the same sources and are the obvious prior art to read before writing a fetcher.

**Every sitting is published twice, and the two texts differ.** Querying the documents for a single sitting returns both a `GroupTypeDesc` of **"דברי הכנסת"** (file prefix `ptm` — the published record) and one of **"סטנוגרמה"** (prefix `st` — the stenogram), alongside an agenda and a table of contents. Comparing the two files for the sitting of 16 July 2025 (the 309th sitting of the 25th Knesset — [record](https://fs.knesset.gov.il/25/Plenum/25_ptm_7716797.doc), [stenogram](https://fs.knesset.gov.il/25/plenum/25_st_7716798.doc)) over an aligned window of the opening debate, the texts agree on about 92% of characters after normalisation. The differences are not only formatting: an interjection attributed to MK Oded Forer appears in the stenogram and not in the record, and a clause spoken by another member appears in the record and not in the stenogram, alongside punctuation and phrasing edits. This is the Israeli counterpart to the Congressional Record's "revise and extend" trap described below — with one decisive difference: **both versions are published**, so a corpus can hold the pair and surface a divergence rather than silently ingesting an edit. Whether the divergence is systematic or an artefact of this one sitting is an open question below.

**The files are parseable, and they hand you the speakers.** The protocol files carry a `.doc` extension but are OOXML (`file` reports "Microsoft Word 2007+"; they unzip to `word/document.xml`) — so an ordinary docx reader works and a legacy binary-`.doc` parser is not needed for recent sittings. More valuable: the text carries inline structural markers — `<< יור >>` (chair), `<< דובר >>` (speaker), `<< דובר_המשך >>` (same speaker continuing), `<< קריאה >>` (interjection), `<< נושא >>` (topic), `<< הצח >>` (bill) — 3,316 marker occurrences in the record file and 3,567 in the stenogram for that one sitting. Speaker attribution, the hardest part of turning a transcript into per-figure statements, is published as data rather than left to inference. (All of this verified first-hand this pass against the two files linked above.)

**Three decades of it are already a research corpus.** The Knesset Corpus (Goldin, Howell, Ordan, Rabinovich and Wintner) covers all plenary and committee protocols from 1992 to 2024 — over 30 million sentences and 384 million tokens — annotated with morpho-syntactic information and named entities, with speaker metadata drawn from a compiled database of members and factions, and distributed through CLARIN's ParlaMint platform ([Language Resources and Evaluation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10579-025-09833-4), [University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/the-knesset-corpus-an-annotated-corpus-of-hebrew-parliamentary-pr/)). The historical backbone need not be re-scraped; its licence terms were not established this pass.

**Broadcast and chamber video.** The Knesset Channel publishes plenum, committee and programme VOD at [knesset.tv](https://www.knesset.tv/vod/) with a [terms-and-privacy page](https://www.knesset.tv/documents/terms-and-privacy/) not read this pass — the C-SPAN lesson from the US section is that this is exactly the document to read before assuming the video is usable. The Knesset's own live and archived streams and its YouTube channel are the alternative routes.

## Hebrew is the technical tax, and most of it is already paid

**The tax.** Hebrew is a morphologically rich language written without vowels, and both properties break naive text matching. A single token can correspond to five English words (`וכשמהמעברה`), and an unvocalised token is routinely ambiguous between unrelated readings — `הקפה` reads as "orbit", "the coffee", or "her perimeter" with no a-priori favourite ([*What's Wrong with Hebrew NLP? And How to Make it Right*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.05453), [*MRL Parsing Without Tears: The Case of Hebrew*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.06970)). Practitioners building Hebrew search hit this first: word-level matching cannot work before morphological segmentation, and Semitic languages are the hardest case for it ([HebMorph: challenges with indexing Hebrew texts](https://code972.com/blog/2010/05/challenges-with-indexing-hebrew-texts-hebmorph-part-1-18)). Any "find every time this figure said X" feature is a morphological-analysis problem in Hebrew where it is a substring problem in English.

**Why that is a moat rather than a wall.** The analysis stack is open and maintained by Israeli nonprofits. [DICTA](https://dicta.org.il/), a non-profit centre for Hebrew text analysis, publishes DictaBERT (with morphological tagging and prefix-segmentation variants), DictaLM 2.0, and [Dicta-LM 3.0](https://dicta.org.il/publications/DictaLM_3_0___Techincal_Report.pdf) — open-weight 24B, 12B and 1.7B models continuously pre-trained on roughly 100 billion Hebrew tokens with 65k context and tool-calling — all on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/dicta-il). There is even a model trained on exactly this corpus: [Knesset-DictaBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.20581), a Hebrew language model for parliamentary proceedings. For speech, [ivrit.ai](https://www.ivrit.ai/) has assembled over 10,000 hours of Hebrew speech from 1,000+ speakers, released for free including commercial use, plus [Whisper models fine-tuned on it](https://huggingface.co/ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v2-tuned) ([dataset paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08720)). A US competitor entering Hebrew pays this cost; an Israeli product picks it up for free — the barrier is asymmetric in this product's favour.

**The claim-detection step has Hebrew groundwork too.** Goldin, Wigderson, Rabinovich and Wintner published a factuality annotation scheme applied to nearly 5,000 manually annotated sentences of *Hebrew parliamentary discourse*, distinguishing utterances that correspond to facts from possibilities and imaginary situations, with experiments in predicting the scheme's features automatically ([RANLP 2025 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26406)). That is the "is this sentence a checkable factual claim?" problem, solved to a research standard, on this exact corpus.

## Israeli legal terrain: defamation is the exposure, copyright is not

The US section below concludes that the live risk is contract and terms-of-service rather than criminal law, and that verdict-style labels are well protected as opinion. **Neither half transfers cleanly.**

Copyright exposure on the corpus is *lower*: §6 of the Copyright Act removes Knesset proceedings from copyright outright (above), where the US analysis had to route around a private archive's terms. Israeli law also has a four-factor fair-use provision (§19) covering study, research, criticism, review, journalism and quotation ([full text, Nevo](https://www.nevo.co.il/law_html/law00/3953.htm)).

Defamation exposure is *higher*. The Prohibition of Defamation Law (חוק איסור לשון הרע) permits statutory damages without the plaintiff proving any loss; the cap was raised in 2012 to NIS 300,000, or NIS 600,000 where malice is shown, with more available on proof of special damages. The truth defence is two-part: the statement must be true **and** there must have been public interest in publishing it — a stricter test than the US "actual malice" standard that underwrites the *Stossel* and *Owens* outcomes below ([Kelly Warner summary of Israeli defamation law](https://kellywarnerlaw.com/israel-defamation-laws/), [Israeli practitioner overview](https://www.rnc.co.il/defamation-law-israel/), academic treatment in [*Defamation Law in Turbulence: Does Israel Need 'Libel Reform'?*](https://www.academia.edu/28460212/Defamation_Law_in_Turbulence_Does_Israel_Need_Libel_Reform_)). These are secondary sources; the statute itself was not read this pass, and the open questions below carry that.

The design implication is the same as the US one but sharper: a computed "this contradicts that" must always ship with both quotes and both sources visible, because in Israel the publisher carries a heavier burden and a public figure suing costs them less to try.

## The US field, as background

The sections that follow were researched against the US market and are kept for their transferable lessons — how fact-checking organisations are funded and how that funding fails, what platform dependence does to a product, and what a corpus source's terms can quietly forbid. Read them as precedent, not as this market.

## Public fact-checking: a field at its plateau, hit by a double funding shock

The [Duke Reporters' Lab 2025 census](https://reporterslab.org/2025/06/19/fact-checkers-persevere-as-politicians-platforms-turn-up-heat/) counts 443 active fact-checking projects worldwide — down about 2% from 2024, after years hovering around 450 — active in 116 countries and 70+ languages. Fact-check article volume fell about 6% year over year (measured via ClaimReview tagging). The growth era is over; the field flatlined around 2020 and is now slightly contracting. (reporterslab.org is egress-blocked from this environment; figures read from search results attributing them to the Duke Reporters' Lab census.)

The funding picture is worse than the headcount. Per the IFCN's [State of the Fact-Checkers 2025 report](https://www.poynter.org/ifcn/2026/state-fact-checkers-2025/) (surveying ~140 organizations — sources report 137 and 141; figures as reported in [Rappler's coverage](https://www.rappler.com/world/global-affairs/state-fact-checkers-report-2025-ifcn/), the Poynter origin pages being egress-blocked in this pass), Meta's third-party program fell from 45.5% of average fact-checker revenue in 2024 to 34.3% in 2025; grants became the largest source at 46.2%. 56.9% of orgs depend on a single funder for at least half their income; 76% call their finances vulnerable or in crisis; 38.3% cut staff in 2025, up from 14.9% the year before. Meta had been [the biggest single funder of fact-checking globally](https://dubawa.org/meta-remains-biggest-funder-of-fact-checking-organisations-globally-report/).

The second shock: the January 2025 US foreign-aid freeze and [USAID's closure on July 1, 2025](https://rootsofdevelopment.org/usaid-freeze-february-2025/). Per [RSF](https://rsf.org/en/usa-trump-s-foreign-aid-freeze-throws-journalism-around-world-chaos), USAID's FY2025 budget had $268M for independent media, and in 2023 it supported 707 non-state outlets — much of the Global South fact-checking ecosystem sat downstream of it. So 2025 removed both of the field's largest funders at once.

## The Meta shutdown and the distribution collapse

Meta [announced in January 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/) it would end third-party fact-checking in the US; the program [ended in the US and Community Notes launched in March 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/03/testing-begins-community-notes-facebook-instagram-threads/) ([NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/social-media/meta-ends-fact-checking-program-community-notes-x-rcna186468), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/01/07/meta-eliminates-third-party-fact-checking-moves-to-community-notes.html)). Notably, Meta kept paying fact-checkers in the EU — [Demagog and AFP still partner with Meta in Poland](https://xyz.pl/poland-unpacked/metas-fact-checking-u-turn-why-europe-remains-a-different-story-1861/) — under Digital Services Act pressure, after the [European Commission warned Meta it must assess risks before dropping fact-checkers in Europe](https://www.euronews.com/next/2025/01/08/meta-needs-to-analyse-risks-if-it-drops-fact-checkers-in-eu-too-commission). Regulation, not market demand, is what holds the paid model up where it survives.

A quieter but comparable blow: [Google killed the ClaimReview fact-check snippet in June 2025](https://www.niemanlab.org/2025/06/google-kills-the-fact-checking-snippet/) as part of "simplifying" search results. Per [Poynter's report on the removal](https://www.poynter.org/ifcn/2025/google-claimreview-fact-checks-snippets-removed/), 250,000+ fact checks carried ClaimReview markup and Google itself estimated ~4 billion annual impressions in 2019; those now render as plain blue links ([Full Fact's reaction](https://fullfact.org/technology/the-web-just-got-a-little-harder-to-trust/)).

The uncomfortable finding for the professional model: crowd checking works. A [PNAS study](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2503413122) found a Community Note cuts a post's reposts by 46% and likes by 44% within 48 hours, and [University of Rochester research](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/crowdsourcing-fact-checking-community-notes-social-media-676142/) found noted posts were 32% more likely to be deleted by their authors. Known limits remain coverage and speed — only 6,757 of ~40,000 proposed notes in one tracked window were rated helpful and attached.

## Civic-data and transparency platforms: no sustainable business model found

The segment's history is a warning. The [Sunlight Foundation, the archetypal government-transparency tech org, closed after 15 years](https://thefulcrum.us/governance-legislation/sunlight-foundation), its board concluding its role was "no longer essential." A [Knight Foundation study of 177 civic-tech companies](https://knightfoundation.org/press/releases/new-report-civic-tech-companies-make-gains-struggle-with-sustainable-business-models/) found the sector makes civic gains but consistently struggles to find sustainable business models. [Vote Smart still operates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_Smart) (its Political Courage Test of candidate positions continues), but ran expenses of $1.54M against $1.28M revenue in 2024 per [Charity Navigator](https://www.charitynavigator.org/ein/850364261), and [incumbents increasingly refuse to answer its position surveys](https://www.reviewjournal.com/news/incumbents-snub-political-courage-test/). Grant-funded civic tech outside the US faces the same USAID-shaped hole: [Nigeria's civic-tech sector calls it an existential crisis](https://insights.techcabal.com/nigeria-civic-tech-funding-crisis/), and [Carnegie documents shrinking funding for civic actors globally](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/shrinking-civic-space-digital-funding-and-legitimacy-in-a-post-truth-era?lang=en).

Politwoops is the cautionary tale for platform-dependent accountability corpora: after a decade archiving 500,000+ deleted politician tweets, [ProPublica froze it in February 2023](https://www.propublica.org/article/politwoops-deleted-tweets-twitter-politicians-musk) because post-Musk API changes broke deletion tracking ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/02/after-a-decade-of-tracking-politicians-deleted-tweets-politwoops-is-no-more/)).

## Commercial media monitoring: where the money actually is

Analyst estimates of the media-monitoring(-tools) market diverge widely for 2025 — [USD 5.40B (Mordor Intelligence)](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/media-monitoring-market), [USD 6.30B (Fortune Business Insights)](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/media-monitoring-tools-market-104157), [USD 9.49B (Market Research Future)](https://www.marketresearchfuture.com/reports/media-monitoring-tools-market-22515) — so treat the size as "mid-single-digit billions, sources disagree on whose estimate." Key players: Cision, Meltwater, Brandwatch, Talkwalker, Mention ([IMARC](https://www.imarcgroup.com/media-monitoring-tools-market)). Pricing is opaque and enterprise-shaped: Meltwater runs [roughly $15,000–$40,000+/year](https://prowly.com/magazine/meltwater-pricing/), with a [median buyer at ~$25,000/year per Vendr procurement data](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/meltwater); [Cision averages ~$9.5k SMB to ~$75k enterprise](https://authoritytech.io/blog/cision-vs-meltwater-2026).

The closest commercial analogue to a statements corpus already exists: [Factba.se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factba.se), a searchable database of presidential statements, speeches, tweets and interviews, cited 4,000+ times by major outlets, [acquired by FiscalNote in 2021](https://fiscalnote.com/newsroom/fiscalnote-acquires-factsquared) and [folded into Roll Call in 2024](https://fiscalnote.com/newsroom/fiscalnote-announces-us-presidential-election-coverage) — its [Trump transcript search is live at Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/factbase/trump/search/). But the buyer's trajectory tempers the opportunity story: FiscalNote's [ARR fell 21% year-over-year to $84.1M at end-2025](https://fiscalnote.com/newsroom/fiscalnote-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025-financial-results), suggesting policy-intelligence subscriptions are a harder sell than the TAM decks claim.

## AI and automation in fact-checking

The automation stack is mature at the "find and match" stages, not the "verify" stage. [ClaimBuster](https://www.rand.org/research/projects/truth-decay/fighting-disinformation/search/items/claimbuster.html) (UT Arlington) has scored sentences for "check-worthiness" since 2017 and [published the first end-to-end fact-checking pipeline](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol10/p1945-li.pdf): monitor broadcast captions and feeds, score claims, match against existing fact-check repositories. [Full Fact AI](https://fullfact.org/ai/) productized the same triad — claim detection, claim matching (embeddings plus entity recognition and a trained match model), and monitoring — and is [used by 45+ organizations in 30 countries](https://fullfact.ai/); Full Fact [added a two-stage generative-AI system in May 2025](https://newsmachines.beehiiv.com/p/how-full-fact-scales-human-fact-checking-with-ai-1) and [exported its tools to US newsrooms for election coverage](https://www.poynter.org/fact-checking/2025/the-uks-fact-checkers-are-sending-their-ai-to-help-americans-cover-elections/). The consistent framing in [Full Fact's own writing](https://fullfact.org/blog/2025/feb/how-ai-can-help-fact-checkers/) is AI as scaling assistant for human checkers — nobody credible ships automated verdicts. That leaves detection of contradictions *within one speaker's record over time* — as opposed to claim-vs-evidence checking — comparatively unclaimed territory.

## The manual status quo

What "checking what a figure said" looks like today: keyword search over the [C-SPAN Video Library's 230,000+ hours of indexed public-affairs video](https://www.c-span.org/30-years/), which [reshaped opposition research as early as the 2012 campaign](https://swampland.time.com/2011/12/14/the-c-span-campaign-how-online-video-archives-are-changing-the-game-in-2012/); the [GDELT Television Explorer over the Internet Archive's TV News Archive](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-television-api-debuts/) (~2M hours, 5.7B words of closed captions from 150+ stations since 2009, keyword-searchable with CSV export); and paid news archives — [Nexis at ~$26/month for freelancers, Factiva pay-per-article at ~$3](https://businessjournalism.org/2010/09/lexisnexis-news-article-searches/) ([LexisNexis pitches 45+ years of archives to newsrooms](https://www.lexisnexis.com/en-us/professional/research/media.page)). For position *changes* specifically, the manual benchmark is PolitiFact's [Flip-O-Meter](https://www.politifact.com/article/2008/aug/05/introducing-flip-o-meter/), a hand-researched No Flip / Half Flip / Full Flop rating that explicitly [frames consistency analysis as informative rather than a value judgment](https://politifact.com/article/2018/feb/12/principles-truth-o-meter-politifacts-methodology-i/). All of these are per-query, labor-intensive, and none maintains a per-figure longitudinal statement corpus with contradiction detection.

The raw-material pipe has narrowed: X [ended free API access and the Academic Research tier in 2023](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/qa-what-happened-to-academic-research-on-twitter.php), moving to [$100/month for 10,000 tweets and ~$42,000/month enterprise](https://theconversation.com/twitters-new-data-fees-leave-scientists-scrambling-for-funding-or-cutting-research-199238); researchers [documented 250+ public-interest projects jeopardized](https://independenttechresearch.org/letter-twitters-new-api-plans-will-devastate-public-interest-research/), and by 2026 there is [still no academic tier, with pricing now pay-per-usage credits](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/x-twitter-api-2026).

## Where a US statements corpus can lawfully come from

The section above names C-SPAN's Video Library as the practical archive of what figures said on the record, and an earlier form of the legal read below concluded that "government sources (C-SPAN-style proceedings, congressional records, official transcripts) carry none of that contractual exposure, which quietly makes them the safest corpus backbone." That was half right, and the wrong half is the operative one: **the proceedings are public domain, but C-SPAN's archive of them is not an available source.**

C-SPAN's terms of use forbid using "any bot, spider, or other automatic device, or manual process to monitor or copy our Content without prior written permission of C-SPAN (this excludes search engines)" ([C-SPAN Terms and Conditions](https://www.c-span.org/about/termsAndConditions/)). Under the hiQ line of cases below, that is precisely the contract/terms-of-service exposure that is live for a scraper, rather than the CFAA exposure that is not. C-SPAN's copyright policy does put the chamber video in the public domain — "video coverage of the debates originating from the chambers of the U.S. House of Representatives and the U.S. Senate is in the public domain and as such, may be used without restriction or attribution" — while separately refusing unlicensed *commercial* use of any of its programming, attributed or not, and requiring a license for "any use that relies substantially on C-SPAN video to generate revenue" ([C-SPAN Copyright Policy](https://www.c-span.org/classroom/copyright/), [Licensing and Permissions, C-SPAN Video Library](http://www.c-spanvideo.org/videoLibrary/rights.php)). The public-domain status attaches to the proceedings, not to the archive that indexed them. (c-span.org is egress-blocked from this environment; the quoted policy language is read from search results quoting those pages.)

The government publishes the same material itself, with neither problem. The Office of the Clerk's HouseLive carries floor video, floor summaries and the linked Congressional Record text from 2009 to the present, and Congress.gov aggregates House and Senate video ([House History: Congressional Video Footage](https://history.house.gov/Records-and-Research/Archival-Research/Congressional-Footage/), [Congress.gov Video](https://www.congress.gov/video)). GPO's govinfo publishes the daily Congressional Record (the CREC collection) as bulk XML from its Bulk Data Repository and through an API keyed free via api.data.gov, which answers with `X-RateLimit-*` headers and a 429 when a key runs hot — a documented quota rather than a ban on automation ([govinfo Developer Hub](https://www.govinfo.gov/developers), [usgpo/api](https://github.com/usgpo/api)); Congress.gov states that the data its API returns is in the public domain ([Guidance for Developers Using Congress.gov Data Offsite](https://www.congress.gov/help/using-data-offsite)). For broadcast rather than chamber speech, the Internet Archive's TV News Archive plus GDELT's explorers remain the free research route already described under the manual status quo.

**But the Congressional Record is not a transcript.** House rules make it "a substantially verbatim account of remarks made during the proceedings of the House, subject only to technical, grammatical and typographical corrections authorized by the Member making the remarks involved" — and members may additionally revise and extend, printing material in the Record they never spoke on the floor at all ([CRS, *A User's Guide to the Congressional Record*](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/98-265GOV.html); [Laws and Rules for Publication of the Congressional Record, GPO](https://www.govinfo.gov/media/RULES.PDF); [House Practice: Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPRACTICE-104/html/GPO-HPRACTICE-104-16.htm) — those domains are egress-blocked here, language read from search results). For a product whose claim is "here is what this figure said, with the source", a corpus built on the Record inherits an edit the speaker controls: the video is the verbatim record, the Record is the speaker's approved version of it, and a contradiction detected between two Record entries may be a contradiction between two edits.

## US legal and ethical terrain

US courts have been favorable to verdict-style speech. In [Stossel v. Meta](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/10/facebook-defeats-lawsuit-over-its-fact-checking-explanations-stossel-v-meta.htm), a "Partly False" fact-check label was held a protected subjective assessment, not actionable defamation ([Loeb & Loeb summary](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/10/stossel-v-meta-platforms)); [Candace Owens' suit against fact-checker Lead Stories was likewise dismissed](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/post/judge-tosses-suit-by-candace-owens-over-facebook-fact-checks/). Most relevant to an "inconsistency detected" product: courts [protect opinions based on disclosed facts](https://www.rcfp.org/two-courts-reaffirm-protections-opinions-based-disclosed-facts/) — a characterization ("this contradicts that") sitting atop quoted, cited statements is the strongest defamation posture available. The design implication: always show the underlying quotes and sources beside any computed verdict.

On scraping: [hiQ v. LinkedIn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HiQ_Labs_v._LinkedIn) established that scraping publicly accessible data does not violate the CFAA (no criminal exposure), but hiQ ultimately [lost on breach-of-contract grounds and settled with a permanent injunction plus $500,000](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/) — the live risk for a statements scraper is terms-of-service and contract claims from platforms, not prosecution ([Farella Braun analysis](https://www.fbm.com/publications/what-recent-rulings-in-hiq-v-linkedin-and-other-cases-say-about-the-legality-of-data-scraping/)). Government sources carry none of that contractual exposure — but the 2026-08-23 pass corrected the earlier form of this claim, which named "C-SPAN-style proceedings" among them: C-SPAN's own terms bar automated copying outright, so its archive is not a government source for this purpose. The safe backbone is the government's own publishers, with the Congressional Record's non-verbatim caveat attached — see [Where a US statements corpus can lawfully come from](#where-a-us-statements-corpus-can-lawfully-come-from) above.

## Open questions

- Is the divergence between "דברי הכנסת" and the stenogram systematic, and in which direction? One sitting was compared this pass; the answer decides whether a corpus stores both files or one.
- Does the Knesset OData service really exclude per-MK voting and everything before the 18th Knesset? The claim came from a secondary skill listing, not from the API or its documentation.
- Do the Knesset's own site terms, or fs.knesset.gov.il's, restrict automated retrieval? No terms page was located this pass — the C-SPAN lesson says find it before assuming.
- Under what licence is the Knesset Corpus (ParlaMint/CLARIN) redistributable, and does it cover derived commercial products? It would supply 1992–2024 without a re-scrape.
- Are committee protocols structured like plenum ones (same `<< >>` markers, same twin record/stenogram pair)? Only plenum documents were inspected.
- How far back does the OData document coverage actually reach, and in what format are the older files (binary `.doc`, PDF, scans)?
- What do Knesset Channel's terms permit for its plenum/committee VOD, and is there any official archive of pre-2000s chamber audio or video?
- What share of an Israeli figure's contradiction-relevant speech happens outside the Knesset — Channel 12/14 studios, radio, party conferences, Telegram, X? The chamber record covers the chamber only.
- Israeli defamation exposure read from the statute itself (חוק איסור לשון הרע) rather than practitioner summaries: what exactly does the public-interest limb of the truth defence require of an automated verdict?
- Is there Israeli case law on algorithmic or database-generated characterisations of a public figure's statements — or is the nearest precedent still ordinary press defamation?
- What did Meta's exit from third-party fact-checking do to HaMashrokit specifically? Meta's announcements covered the US; the Israeli contract's status is unknown.
